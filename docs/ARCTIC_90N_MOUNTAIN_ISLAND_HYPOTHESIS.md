# Arctic 90°N Mountain-Island Hypothesis

## Status

**Open research hypothesis — not a construction authorization.**

This document records a deliberately extreme idea: investigate whether a permanent artificial mountain/island fixed at the geographic North Pole could become a uniquely stable Earth-observation reference point and whether such a structure would measurably alter local or regional ocean, sea-ice and atmosphere dynamics.

The proposal must be judged by measurement and simulation, not by assumption. If modeling shows unacceptable environmental or geophysical risk, the concept must be rejected or redesigned.

## The proposed geometry

The working concept is a tapered artificial island / mountain built from material excavated from the Arctic seabed near 90°N.

Initial simulation envelope:

- geographic reference point: **90°N**;
- ocean depth used for first-order calculations: about **4 km**;
- emergent height above mean sea level: about **4 km**;
- total vertical scale from seabed to summit: about **8 km**;
- nominal base footprint: about **8 km × 8 km**;
- external geometry: pyramid / truncated-pyramid / broad tapered mountain;
- source-material hypothesis: use local seabed/rock material only if geology, law and environmental assessment show that extraction is permissible and technically possible.

For a perfect 8 km × 8 km pyramid with an 8 km total height, the geometric volume is about **171 km³**. At a representative rock density of 2,700 kg/m³, this corresponds to roughly **4.6 × 10^14 kg** of material (about 460 billion tonnes). A truncated top, foundations, protective layers and voids would change this number substantially.

Therefore an “8 km crater” cannot be treated as a simple visual detail. Conservation of volume, sediment thickness, crustal geology, slope stability and excavation depth must all be calculated. Removing enough material from an 8 km-wide source area could require excavation depths of kilometres, which itself could be geologically unacceptable.

## Why investigate such an extreme idea?

The scientific motivation is not “change the planet and see what happens.” The defensible objective is:

1. obtain a permanent geodetic reference point at 90°N;
2. support vertically distributed sensors from seabed to upper atmosphere;
3. compare fixed measurements against drifting ice platforms and satellite products;
4. quantify how a large fixed obstacle would perturb currents, sea-ice drift, mixing and the atmospheric boundary layer;
5. learn whether those perturbations are local, regional or negligible at larger scales;
6. determine whether a lower-impact architecture could deliver the same scientific value.

## Existing near-pole observations — important correction

The project must not claim that no fixed measurements have ever existed near the North Pole.

The **North Pole Environmental Observatory (NPEO)** maintained bottom-anchored oceanographic moorings on the Pole Abyssal Plain from 2001 to 2010, typically about 50–80 km from the geographic pole, in water about 4.3 km deep. Those moorings measured current speed/direction, temperature, salinity, pressure and ice draft through the year. Since 2010, NPEO reports a bottom-pressure recorder on the seafloor at **90°N**.

What is still missing is a permanent, full-stack, land-like observatory exactly at 90°N combining a stable geodetic surface reference with seabed, full water-column, ice, atmosphere and communications infrastructure.

## What current speeds do we actually know?

There is **no honest single 12-month table of exact surface and seabed current speed at 90°N that can be stated as a universal climatology**. Currents vary with eddies, storms, ice conditions, stratification and depth.

However, observations close to the pole provide useful scale estimates:

### Upper ocean / background flow

Near-pole measurements and Arctic buoy studies show that background upper-ocean currents are commonly of order **a few centimetres per second**. Aagaard et al. (2008), using a mooring about 55 km from the pole, report Arctic eddy speeds typically **0.1–0.4 m/s**, roughly ten times background speed. That implies background currents of order **0.01–0.04 m/s (1–4 cm/s)** in the observed layers.

Eddies and storm-driven events can therefore temporarily dominate the background flow.

### Deep / abyssal flow

Aagaard (1981) reports that abyssal flow in the Arctic Ocean is generally **less than 0.01 m/s (1 cm/s)**, with energetic episodes of about **0.02–0.04 m/s (2–4 cm/s)**, often lasting or recurring on multi-day time scales. Near the pole, deeper overflow episodes can locally exceed these values.

### Recent full-year deep measurements

A modern AWI/PANGAEA mooring in the Amundsen Basin (CAO2-01, 2023–2024) includes current meters near **215 m and 3,796 m** and covers roughly one full year. As of August 2026, the raw archive is still under a data moratorium until October 2026, so this project must not invent monthly current values before the actual files are available.

### What a future 90°N station should publish

For every month it should report at minimum:

- 1 m / under-ice current speed and direction;
- 10 m, 50 m, 100 m, 250 m, 500 m, 1,000 m, 2,000 m, 3,000 m and near-bottom current vectors;
- median, mean, 5th/95th percentile and maximum event speed;
- ice-drift speed separately from water-current speed;
- turbulence, vertical velocity and shear;
- uncertainty and calibration state for each sensor.

That would produce the full-year comparison the project actually needs.

## Could the mountain “calm the ocean”?

**Possibly locally; globally this is unproven and currently unlikely.**

A structure extending from a ~4 km seabed to the surface would be a complete-depth obstacle. It would almost certainly alter local flow by creating some combination of:

- blocking and diversion;
- wake formation;
- recirculation zones;
- turbulence;
- upwelling/downwelling;
- internal-wave generation;
- changes in stratification and mixing;
- altered sea-ice stress and pressure-ridge formation;
- sediment transport and scour.

But “slower current” cannot be assumed. An obstacle can also **accelerate water around its flanks**, generate eddies and intensify local shear. Therefore the correct research question is not “will it calm the ocean?” but:

> **How will an 8 km-scale, full-depth obstacle redistribute kinetic energy, transport and mixing in the central Arctic Ocean?**

This must be answered first with bathymetry-resolving CFD/ocean models and ice–ocean coupling models, then with scaled physical tests.

## Tornadoes and extreme weather

The ocean does influence weather and climate. NOAA research links sea-surface temperature patterns, large-scale atmospheric modes and tropical convection to changes in tornado probability on subseasonal scales.

However, there is currently **no scientific basis for claiming that one artificial island at 90°N would reduce tornadoes globally**. Tornado formation depends on atmospheric instability, moisture, vertical wind shear and storm-scale dynamics.

A 90°N mountain could alter the **local and perhaps regional atmospheric boundary layer**, winds, clouds and heat exchange. Whether those changes propagate far enough to influence mid-latitude severe weather is a testable modeling question, not an established effect.

Therefore the project should simulate:

- local wind-field disturbance around a 4 km-high mountain;
- orographic cloud and precipitation formation;
- wave propagation into the upper atmosphere;
- Arctic pressure-pattern changes;
- possible teleconnections;
- whether any detected signal survives natural variability.

## Sea ice — the immediate mechanical enemy

The first-order operational threat is moving and deforming sea ice, not ordinary ocean current speed.

A fixed island would interrupt natural ice drift. Possible consequences include:

- ice pile-up and pressure ridges on the upstream side;
- accelerated flow of ice around the flanks;
- persistent leads or open-water zones downstream;
- altered local heat flux between ocean and atmosphere;
- mechanical abrasion and impact loading;
- habitat changes.

These effects could be scientifically valuable to observe but environmentally dangerous if underestimated.

## Tectonic plates and crustal loading

The hypothesis that the island could meaningfully “press” tectonic plates or control continental motion is **not supported by current plate-tectonic physics**.

Plate motion is driven primarily by forces associated with mantle convection, gravity and subduction/slab pull. Ocean currents do not drive tectonic plates.

Relocating on the order of 10^14–10^15 kg of rock could nevertheless create **local crustal loading/unloading, flexure, stress redistribution and possibly induced seismic effects**, especially if deep excavation intersects faults or unstable crust. That makes geomechanical modeling mandatory.

The scientifically valid question is therefore:

> Could excavation and redistribution of hundreds of billions of tonnes of rock produce measurable **local** crustal deformation or seismicity?

—not whether it can steer global tectonic plates.

## Centimetres do not become hundreds of kilometres

A centimetre of measurement error at the surface does **not geometrically become hundreds of kilometres of position error in space**.

The real amplification problem is different and still important: centimetre-scale bias in sea-ice freeboard can propagate into decimetre-scale uncertainty in derived ice thickness, and when integrated over very large areas can create substantial uncertainty in total ice volume or mass.

The project must preserve units and uncertainty propagation explicitly so that a visually dramatic statement never replaces a quantitative one.

## Could this improve a true 3D Earth model?

Yes — but as one reference node, not as the sole foundation of a 3D Earth model.

Modern Earth shape, terrain and gravity models already use GNSS, satellite altimetry, gravimetry, radar, lidar and geodesy with high precision. The special value of a 90°N island would be in **polar ocean–ice–atmosphere validation**, especially where the surface is moving, seasonal and difficult to revisit with identical geometry.

A fixed site could host:

- permanent GNSS monuments;
- radar/laser calibration targets;
- absolute gravity measurements;
- tide/sea-level references;
- seabed pressure and geodesy;
- full water-column ADCP/CTD arrays;
- meteorology, aerosols and radiation sensors;
- calibrated optical/thermal cameras;
- satellite and aircraft calibration campaigns.

## Deep-ocean excavation machinery — research concept

If the geology and environmental studies ever justified moving local material, humanity would need a new class of deep-ocean construction systems. The project may study concepts such as:

- remotely operated seabed excavation vehicles;
- autonomous dredging/rock-cutting systems;
- enclosed sediment-handling systems to minimize plumes;
- subsea material conveyors or lifting systems;
- autonomous survey and geotechnical robots;
- continuous environmental monitoring around any test site.

These are **technology research topics**, not instructions to excavate the Arctic seabed before international authorization and environmental assessment.

## Decision rule

The project can openly state the ambition:

> **If rigorous modeling, independent review and staged Arctic demonstrations show that a permanent 90°N artificial island provides unique scientific value with acceptable environmental and geophysical risk, then construction should be considered internationally.**

But the project must also state the reverse:

> **If the same measurements can be obtained with lower-impact fixed seabed systems, autonomous moorings, mobile platforms or satellites, or if the island creates unacceptable risk, the full-scale island should not be built.**

That is not weakness. It is the scientific test that makes the proposal credible.

## Required simulations before any construction decision

1. high-resolution bathymetry and crustal structure at 90°N;
2. 3D ocean circulation with and without the island;
3. sea-ice drift, ridging and mechanical loading;
4. sediment transport and excavation plume models;
5. local crustal loading/unloading and seismic hazard;
6. atmospheric flow over a 4 km-high polar mountain;
7. regional weather and climate sensitivity ensembles;
8. ecosystem and biogeochemical impact;
9. comparison against lower-impact observatory designs;
10. international legal, environmental and safety review.
