# Arctic 90°N Artificial Mountain — Preliminary Risk Assessment

**Status:** DEMO / first-pass screening only. This is not a construction design, CFD result, geotechnical certification, tsunami forecast, or environmental approval.

Public interactive demo in the Polar project:

- https://terraforming-planet.github.io/Polar-Sun-Moon-Analysis/arctic-90n/risk-simulation.html

## Baseline geometry used in the first test

The first interactive model starts from the current concept:

- location: geographic North Pole, 90°N;
- nominal base width: **8 km × 8 km**;
- summit width: **0.5 km** in the demo;
- total height: **8 km**;
- water depth in the baseline: **4 km**;
- therefore roughly **4 km below sea level + 4 km above sea level**;
- bulk rock density used for screening: **2700 kg/m³**;
- seawater density: **1025 kg/m³**.

For that square-frustum geometry the first-pass calculator gives approximately:

- total rock volume: **182 km³**;
- submerged rock volume: **154.75 km³**;
- total rock mass: **4.91 × 10^14 kg**;
- effective mass after buoyancy of the submerged section: **3.33 × 10^14 kg**;
- average effective pressure over an ideal 8 km × 8 km base: **~51 MPa**;
- mean flank angle: **~64.9° from horizontal**.

These values immediately show that the 8 km footprint is an aggressive geometry. A 65° average flank is not something that should be assumed stable for loose excavated seabed material. A real concept would need a much wider footprint, staged terraces, engineered confinement, competent rock, cellular/buttressed construction, or another architecture entirely.

## Material-source / excavation sanity check

If the same **182 km³** of material were obtained from one circular seabed excavation, the average excavation depth would be approximately:

| Excavation diameter | Mean depth required for 182 km³ |
|---:|---:|
| 8 km | ~3,621 m |
| 20 km | ~579 m |
| 40 km | ~145 m |
| 80 km | ~36 m |
| 100 km | ~23 m |

This means the original idea of an **8 km-wide source crater cannot supply the mountain without an extraordinarily deep excavation**. The source area must be treated as a separate geotechnical and environmental problem, not as a harmless by-product of construction.

## The most important catastrophe mechanism: rapid mass movement

A stable structure built slowly does **not** generate a tsunami merely by existing. The critical tsunami scenario is a **rapid displacement of a large volume of water** caused by events such as:

- collapse of one mountain flank;
- failure of the seabed foundation;
- collapse of a deep excavation wall;
- a submarine landslide triggered by construction or a natural earthquake;
- a large rock mass falling into the ocean.

NOAA states that landslide-generated tsunami generation depends on the amount of material, its velocity, and the depth of motion. Near the source, landslide tsunamis can be very large and may reach nearby shores with little warning. USGS likewise documents destructive tsunami generated by rapid submarine and subaerial landslides.

Official references:

- NOAA — Tsunami Generation: Landslides: https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/tsunamis/tsunami-generation-landslides
- NOAA/NCEI — Global Historical Tsunami Data: https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/natural-hazards/tsunamis-earthquakes-volcanoes/tsunamis/global-historical-data
- USGS — How do landslides cause tsunamis?: https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/how-do-landslides-cause-tsunamis
- USGS — Submarine Landslides: https://www.usgs.gov/centers/pcmsc/science/submarine-landslides

### Screening collapse volumes

For the ~182 km³ baseline mountain:

- 0.1% rapid failure = **~0.182 km³**;
- 1% rapid failure = **~1.82 km³**;
- 10% rapid failure = **~18.2 km³**.

These numbers are **not tsunami heights**. Wave height cannot be inferred from volume alone. A coupled landslide–water model must include failure geometry, acceleration, duration, density, water depth, bathymetry and ice cover.

At 4 km water depth, the shallow-water long-wave speed `sqrt(g h)` is roughly **198 m/s (~713 km/h)**. This is only a propagation-speed scale after a long wave exists; it says nothing by itself about how high the wave would be.

## Risk register

### 1. Mountain / excavation collapse — consequence: extreme

A rapid multi-km³ failure is the scenario most capable of generating a dangerous local or regional tsunami. It is a hard design gate: no physical construction should proceed until slope stability and coupled tsunami models bound this risk.

### 2. Seabed bearing capacity and settlement — priority: high

The baseline average effective base pressure is ~51 MPa. Actual bearing capacity depends on sediment thickness, pore pressure, rock quality, faults, load distribution and time-dependent settlement. The seabed must be surveyed before any structural conclusion.

USACE maintains separate engineering guidance for slope stability and foundation design because simple geometry and compressive-strength comparisons are insufficient.

- USACE EM 1110-2-1902, Slope Stability: https://www.publications.usace.army.mil/USACE-Publications/Engineer-Manuals/
- USACE EM 1110-1-1905, Geotechnical Design of Shallow Foundations on Soils: same official publications library.

### 3. Pack-ice compression, ridging and keels — priority: high

A fixed island would obstruct drifting pack ice. NSIDC and NOAA describe pressure ridges as the product of converging floes driven by wind and currents. Ridges can extend both above and below the waterline and materially alter ice thickness and momentum transfer.

The island could therefore create persistent upstream compression, local ridging/rafting, leads in its wake and very large horizontal loads near the waterline.

- NSIDC pressure ridge: https://nsidc.org/learn/cryosphere-glossary/pressure-ridge
- NOAA Arctic, landfast sea ice: https://arctic.noaa.gov/report-card/report-card-2018/landfast-sea-ice-in-a-changing-arctic/

### 4. Hydrodynamic drag, wake and scour — priority: high

The island should not be assumed to simply “calm” the ocean. Flow may slow upstream but accelerate around flanks; separation and vortices may increase local turbulence and seabed scour. This needs CFD plus sediment transport modeling.

The interactive demo includes a **very simple particle-deflection visualization** only. It is intentionally labeled as not CFD.

### 5. Local tsunami/flooding vs global sea-level change

The **global mean sea-level displacement from the static volume is small** compared with the catastrophic local-wave scenario. In the baseline model, if all ~154.75 km³ of submerged mountain material were added to the ocean from outside the basin, the global mean sea-level equivalent is only about **0.43 mm** using ~361 million km² of ocean area.

If the material is excavated from the same seabed, the global volume balance changes again because excavation creates additional water-filled space. Therefore a global flood is not the main first-order concern from static displacement. **Rapid failure and local/regional tsunami are much more important hazards.**

### 6. Local crustal stress / induced seismicity — priority: research

Moving hundreds of billions of tonnes changes local loading while excavation unloads another area. USGS research on reservoir-induced seismicity shows that surface loading and changes in pore pressure can alter failure likelihood on faults that are already stressed. This does not imply that the island would control global plate tectonics.

- USGS: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/tectonic-state-its-significance-and-characterization-assessment-seismic-effects

### 7. Atmospheric and weather effects — priority: research

A new 4 km-high obstacle would create substantial local orographic effects: altered wind, clouds, turbulence and mountain-wave generation. It is scientifically legitimate to test whether downstream circulation changes, but there is **no basis to claim that one island would globally suppress tornadoes or stabilize weather**.

### 8. Environmental damage during construction — priority: high

Potential mechanisms include:

- sediment plumes;
- burial/removal of benthic habitat;
- altered nutrient and carbon cycling;
- noise and vibration;
- contaminant release from disturbed sediment;
- chronic scour and changed ice ecology.

Environmental impact assessment is therefore a design gate, not an afterthought.

## Risk logic for TerraWater AI

The system should record each claim with explicit evidence status:

- `OBSERVED` — measured or documented event;
- `DERIVED` — calculated directly from stated assumptions;
- `MODEL_ESTIMATE` — result of numerical model;
- `SCREENING_SCENARIO` — deliberately simplified worst/best case;
- `UNKNOWN` — cannot be quantified from current data.

The current 3D page is `SCREENING_SCENARIO`. It is useful for discovering which calculations matter next, not for proving safety.

## Next simulation stack

The next real technical program should be staged:

1. **Bathymetry/geology digital twin** — exact 90°N seabed and candidate source areas.
2. **Geotechnical FEM** — foundation, excavation slope, settlement and failure surfaces.
3. **CFD** — currents, wake, pressure, vertical mixing and scour.
4. **Sea-ice mechanics** — drift, compression, ridging, keels and impact loads.
5. **Landslide + tsunami model** — 0.1%, 1%, 10% rapid-failure scenarios and excavation-wall collapse.
6. **Atmospheric model** — local and regional orographic effects.
7. **Coupled sensitivity study** — compare 8 km, 12 km, 16 km, 24 km and wider bases.
8. **Environmental model** — sediment plume and ecosystem disturbance.

## Current first-pass verdict

**Do not reject the scientific objective, but do not trust the 8 km footprint yet.** The first calculations suggest that the current geometry is too steep and concentrates too much load to treat as a plausible loose-seabed-material mountain without major redesign. The useful next move is exactly what the demo is for: widen the base, change the top, reduce slopes, spread excavation over a much larger source area, and quantify how every redesign changes tsunami, ice and hydrodynamic risk.
